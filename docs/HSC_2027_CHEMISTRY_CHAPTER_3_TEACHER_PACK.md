# HSC 2027 Chemistry — Chapter 3 Teacher Pack
## 1st Paper, Chapter 3: Periodic Properties of Elements and Chemical Bonds

**Status:** Original draft for academic review. This is a proposed teaching sequence, not an official NCTB subchapter list or a substitute for the prescribed textbook. Confirm exact scope, terminology, diagrams, exceptions and required calculations against the current NCTB textbook and latest official board notice before teaching or publication.

## A. Chapter delivery specification

**Suggested duration:** 4–5 teaching weeks, adjustable to the academy timetable.  
**Prerequisites:** Chapter 1 laboratory safety; Chapter 2 ions and qualitative evidence; atomic number, mass number, protons, neutrons, electrons, shells, valence electrons, simple ions and chemical formulae.  
**Core outcome:** Learners connect atomic structure and effective nuclear attraction to periodic trends, then use electron arrangements and electrostatic models to explain bonding, molecular shape, polarity and selected physical properties.

### Measurable learning outcomes

By the end of this chapter, a learner should be able to:

1. Locate an element from its atomic number and relate its position to shell structure and valence-electron arrangement for the elements covered in the prescribed course.
2. Write and interpret electron configurations using the notation and filling rules required by the textbook.
3. Explain the roles of nuclear charge, shielding and distance in periodic trends.
4. Compare atomic and ionic radii, first ionisation energies, electron-affinity tendencies and electronegativities using supplied data or periodic position.
5. Explain broad trends across a period and down a group, while recognising that some properties have exceptions and depend on definitions and measurement conditions.
6. Distinguish ionic, covalent, coordinate covalent and metallic bonding at the level prescribed by the textbook.
7. Draw simple Lewis electron-dot representations and use them to explain bond formation and valence-electron sharing or transfer.
8. Predict common molecular shapes using a simple electron-pair repulsion model when required by the syllabus.
9. Distinguish bond polarity from molecular polarity and explain how shape affects the net dipole of a molecule.
10. Relate bonding and intermolecular attractions to selected properties such as melting point, boiling point, solubility and electrical conductivity without overgeneralising.

## B. Suggested lesson sequence

### Lesson 1 — Atomic structure, electron arrangement and periodic-table organisation
- Revisit atomic number, nuclear charge, shells, subshells and valence electrons.
- Introduce the modern periodic table as an arrangement by increasing atomic number.
- Connect periods with occupied principal shells and groups with related valence-electron patterns for representative groups.
- Practise configurations for the first 20 elements and any additional elements explicitly required by the prescribed text.
- Explain that transition elements and heavier elements may require more careful configuration rules; do not force a simplified main-group pattern onto every element.

**Teacher demonstration:** Give learners element cards containing atomic number and configuration. Ask them to sort by period/group and justify the placement.

### Lesson 2 — Shielding, effective nuclear attraction and atomic/ionic radius
- Define shielding qualitatively: inner electrons reduce the attraction experienced by outer electrons.
- Explain effective nuclear attraction as the net attraction felt by an electron after shielding and distance are considered.
- Across a period, atomic radius generally decreases as nuclear charge increases while added electrons enter the same principal shell.
- Down a group, atomic radius generally increases because additional occupied shells place valence electrons farther from the nucleus.
- Compare cations and their parent atoms (cations are generally smaller) and anions and their parent atoms (anions are generally larger).
- For isoelectronic species, explain that the species with more protons is generally smaller.

**Important qualification:** Radius is not a single directly measured boundary; covalent, metallic and ionic radii are defined in different ways. Compare like with like and use the textbook's stated convention.

### Lesson 3 — Ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity
- Define first ionisation energy as the energy needed to remove an electron from a gaseous atom in its ground state.
- Explain the broad increase across a period and decrease down a group using effective nuclear attraction, distance and shielding.
- Introduce common first-ionisation-energy irregularities (for example, differences associated with subshell stability and electron pairing) only to the depth required by the prescribed text.
- Explain electron affinity as an energy change when a gaseous atom accepts an electron; make clear that sign conventions vary between textbooks and data tables.
- Define electronegativity as a relative tendency of an atom in a bond to attract shared electrons. It is not the same quantity as electron affinity or ionisation energy.
- Compare trends cautiously; avoid presenting every element as following a perfectly smooth sequence.

**Data activity:** Learners rank a small supplied set of elements, then support each ranking with shell, shielding and nuclear-attraction reasoning.

### Lesson 4 — Ionic and covalent bonding; Lewis representations
- Explain ionic bonding as electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions after electron transfer; avoid describing the bond as merely the act of transfer.
- Explain covalent bonding as sharing of electron pair(s) between atoms.
- Introduce single, double and triple covalent bonds and relate bond order qualitatively to bond length and strength.
- Use Lewis symbols and dot-cross diagrams for simple molecules and ionic compounds.
- Introduce coordinate covalent bonding as a shared pair donated by one atom, if included in the prescribed text.
- Discuss the octet rule as a useful model, not a universal law. Note that hydrogen follows a duet and some molecules are exceptions.

**Teacher check:** Require learners to show valence electrons and charges clearly; a correct formula alone does not demonstrate a correct bonding explanation.

### Lesson 5 — Metallic bonding, bond properties and structure–property links
- Model metallic bonding as positive metal centres associated with delocalised electrons.
- Relate mobile electrons to electrical and thermal conductivity in metals.
- Explain malleability and ductility qualitatively through the ability of layers to shift while metallic attraction remains.
- Compare giant ionic lattices, simple molecular substances, giant covalent structures and metallic structures where required.
- Explain why ionic solids commonly have high melting points but do not conduct as solids; molten or aqueous ions can move and carry charge.
- Explain why simple molecular substances often have relatively low melting/boiling points because intermolecular attractions are overcome, not because covalent bonds inside each molecule are necessarily weak.

### Lesson 6 — Molecular shape and electron-pair repulsion
- Introduce the idea that electron domains around a central atom arrange to reduce repulsion.
- Practise common shapes and approximate angles only where required by the textbook: linear, trigonal planar, tetrahedral, trigonal pyramidal and bent.
- Distinguish bonding pairs and lone pairs; explain that lone pairs generally repel more strongly than bonding pairs in the simple model.
- Use physical models or teacher-drawn diagrams; state that the model is a useful approximation, not a literal photograph of electron clouds.

**Scope control:** Do not add advanced hybridisation, molecular orbital theory or complex geometry beyond the prescribed HSC depth unless clearly labelled as optional enrichment.

### Lesson 7 — Bond polarity, molecular polarity and intermolecular forces
- Explain unequal sharing of a covalent pair through electronegativity difference and partial charges.
- Distinguish a polar bond from a polar molecule: molecular shape determines whether bond dipoles cancel.
- Use simple examples such as CO₂ and H₂O to compare symmetric cancellation with a bent arrangement.
- Introduce London dispersion forces, dipole–dipole attractions and hydrogen bonding where prescribed.
- Clarify that hydrogen bonding is a relatively strong intermolecular attraction in the relevant context, not the same as an ordinary covalent bond within a molecule.
- Relate intermolecular attractions to boiling point and other properties using controlled comparisons.

### Lesson 8 — Integrated revision, data interpretation and assessment workshop
- Mixed periodic-trend ranking questions with explanations.
- Electron-configuration and Lewis-structure practice.
- Bond-type identification and structure–property comparisons.
- Molecular-shape and polarity questions using supplied diagrams.
- Short numerical/data questions only where the prescribed textbook requires them.
- Correct misconceptions using evidence and a structured answer method: state the trend/model, identify the relevant structural cause, then apply it to the specific species.

## C. Core concept notes for teachers

### 1. Explaining periodic trends

Use the same three explanatory variables repeatedly:
- **Nuclear charge:** number of protons; greater positive charge can increase attraction.
- **Shielding:** inner electrons reduce the attraction experienced by outer electrons.
- **Distance:** electrons farther from the nucleus generally experience weaker attraction.

A trend explanation should name the relevant comparison. “It increases because it is on the right” is incomplete; learners should explain what changes in nuclear charge, shielding and/or distance.

### 2. First ionisation energy

For a gaseous atom:
`X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻`

The first ionisation energy is the energy required for this process. It is always an energy input for an isolated gaseous atom. Do not confuse it with removing an electron from an ion (successive ionisation energy) or with electron affinity.

### 3. Electron affinity and electronegativity

- Electron affinity concerns an isolated gaseous atom accepting an electron and is expressed as an energy change.
- Electronegativity describes an atom's relative attraction for shared electrons in a chemical bond.
- Different sources may use different sign conventions for electron affinity; read the table heading and units before interpreting values.

### 4. Bonding models

| Model | Main idea | Typical teaching example |
|---|---|---|
| Ionic | Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions in a lattice | NaCl |
| Covalent | Shared electron pair(s) between atoms | H₂, H₂O |
| Coordinate covalent | Shared pair supplied by one atom | Use only examples prescribed in the text |
| Metallic | Positive metal centres with delocalised electrons | Copper or aluminium |

These are explanatory models. Real bonding can have varying degrees of ionic and covalent character.

### 5. Molecular polarity

A polar bond does not guarantee a polar molecule. Learners must consider:
1. Whether the bonds are polar.
2. The three-dimensional arrangement of those bonds.
3. Whether the individual bond dipoles cancel.

For example, CO₂ has polar C=O bonds but a linear symmetric arrangement, so the bond dipoles cancel. H₂O has polar O–H bonds and a bent arrangement, so the dipoles do not cancel.

## D. Common misconceptions and correction prompts

| Misconception | Correction prompt |
|---|---|
| Atomic radius always decreases smoothly across every period | Ask which radius definition and elements are being compared; teach the broad trend and relevant exceptions. |
| Every property has exactly the same periodic trend | Compare the definitions of radius, ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity. |
| Electron affinity and electronegativity mean the same thing | One is an energy change for an isolated gaseous atom; the other is a relative bonding property. |
| Ionic bonding is simply electron transfer | Ask what force holds the resulting ions together in the solid. |
| A molecule with polar bonds must be polar | Draw the shape and consider dipole cancellation. |
| Covalent bonds break when a simple molecular liquid boils | Distinguish intermolecular attractions from covalent bonds within molecules. |
| The octet rule has no exceptions | Compare hydrogen's duet and examples identified in the prescribed textbook. |
| All ionic compounds conduct electricity | Ask whether the ions are fixed in a solid lattice or mobile in a melt/solution. |

## E. Original practice questions

### Part 1 — Multiple-choice questions

**1. Which factor most directly explains the general decrease in atomic radius across a period?**

A. A decrease in proton number  
B. An increase in effective nuclear attraction with electrons entering the same principal shell  
C. The addition of a completely new occupied shell at each step  
D. A complete loss of shielding

**Answer: B.** Proton number increases across a period while added electrons generally enter the same principal shell, so the outer electrons are attracted more strongly overall.

**2. Which species is generally the smallest in the isoelectronic set O²⁻, F⁻, Na⁺ and Mg²⁺?**

A. O²⁻  
B. F⁻  
C. Na⁺  
D. Mg²⁺

**Answer: D.** All have ten electrons. Mg²⁺ has the greatest nuclear charge (12 protons), so it attracts the same electron cloud most strongly.

**3. Which statement about first ionisation energy is correct?**

A. It is the energy released when an atom gains an electron.  
B. It is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom.  
C. It measures the polarity of a covalent bond.  
D. It is the energy needed to melt an ionic solid.

**Answer: B.** The process is X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻.

**4. Which statement best distinguishes electronegativity from electron affinity?**

A. Both are always measured in the same units.  
B. Electronegativity is a relative bonding property; electron affinity is an energy change for gaseous atom electron gain.  
C. Electron affinity applies only to molecules.  
D. Electronegativity is the energy needed to remove an electron.

**Answer: B.** The quantities are related to electron attraction but have different definitions.

**5. What is the main force holding Na⁺ and Cl⁻ together in crystalline sodium chloride?**

A. Hydrogen bonding  
B. Metallic bonding  
C. Electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions  
D. Attraction between neutral molecules only

**Answer: C.** The ionic lattice is held together by electrostatic attraction.

**6. Which molecule has polar bonds but is non-polar overall because of its symmetrical linear shape?**

A. H₂O  
B. NH₃  
C. CO₂  
D. HCl

**Answer: C.** The two C=O bond dipoles are equal and opposite in linear CO₂ and cancel.

**7. Why can solid sodium chloride not conduct electricity as effectively as molten sodium chloride?**

A. The solid contains no charged particles.  
B. The ions are fixed in position in the solid but mobile in the melt.  
C. The covalent bonds disappear on melting.  
D. Electrons become the only charge carriers in the melt.

**Answer: B.** Mobile ions are needed to carry charge through the material.

**8. Which statement about boiling a simple molecular substance is generally correct?**

A. All covalent bonds inside its molecules must break.  
B. Intermolecular attractions are overcome; covalent bonds within molecules usually remain intact.  
C. The atoms become ions.  
D. The substance becomes a metal.

**Answer: B.** Boiling separates molecules from one another rather than normally breaking their internal covalent bonds.

### Part 2 — Short-answer questions with marking guidance

**1. Explain why atomic radius generally increases down a group.**  
*Suggested points (3):*
- Each step down adds an occupied principal electron shell (1).
- The valence electrons are, on average, farther from the nucleus (1).
- Increased shielding helps offset the greater nuclear charge, so the outer electron cloud is generally larger (1).

**2. Explain why a cation is generally smaller than its neutral atom.**  
*Suggested points (3):*
- Electron(s) are removed (1).
- Electron–electron repulsion may decrease and the outer shell may be lost in some cases (1).
- The remaining electrons experience stronger effective attraction per electron, giving a smaller radius (1).

**3. Distinguish ionisation energy, electron affinity and electronegativity.**  
*Suggested points (3):*
- Ionisation energy: energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom (1).
- Electron affinity: energy change when a gaseous atom gains an electron (1).
- Electronegativity: relative tendency of an atom in a bond to attract shared electrons (1).

**4. Describe two differences between ionic and covalent bonding.**  
*Suggested points (4):*
- Ionic bonding involves electrostatic attraction between oppositely charged ions; covalent bonding involves shared electron pairs (2).
- Give one correct example of each or explain a relevant structural/property difference (2).

**5. Why is CO₂ non-polar overall even though each C=O bond is polar?**  
*Suggested points (3):*
- Each C=O bond has a dipole due to unequal sharing (1).
- CO₂ is linear and symmetrical (1).
- The two equal, opposite bond dipoles cancel (1).

**6. Explain why metals conduct electricity and are malleable.**  
*Suggested points (4):*
- Metals contain delocalised/mobile electrons (1).
- These electrons carry charge through the structure (1).
- Metallic bonding is non-directional in the simple model (1).
- Layers can shift while attraction between metal centres and delocalised electrons remains (1).

### Part 3 — Application and creative-question scenarios

**Scenario A: Comparing three elements**

A learner is given three main-group elements from the same period, labelled P, Q and R, with atomic numbers increasing in the order P < Q < R. Their valence electrons enter the same principal shell.

a. State the broad trend in atomic radius from P to R.  
b. Explain the trend using nuclear charge and shielding.  
c. State the broad trend in first ionisation energy.  
d. Give one reason why a measured sequence may contain a local exception.

*Suggested marking guide (6):*
- a. Radius generally decreases (1).
- b. Nuclear charge increases while shielding changes comparatively little across the same shell, increasing effective attraction (2).
- c. First ionisation energy generally increases (1).
- d. Subshell arrangement or electron-pair repulsion can cause an irregularity; accept a relevant textbook example (2).

**Scenario B: Ionic crystal and aqueous solution**

A small amount of an ionic solid is placed in a dry test circuit and shows no useful conductivity. When a suitable soluble sample is dissolved in water, the solution conducts.

a. Explain the observation for the solid.  
b. Explain why the solution conducts.  
c. Predict the behaviour of the molten ionic compound.  
d. Identify the charge carriers in the solution.

*Suggested marking guide (6):*
- a. Ions are held in fixed lattice positions (2).
- b. Dissolved ions are mobile and can carry charge (2).
- c. The melt conducts because ions become mobile (1).
- d. Both cations and anions carry charge (1).

**Scenario C: Two molecules**

Molecule X has two polar bonds arranged linearly and symmetrically. Molecule Y has polar bonds around a central atom in a bent shape.

a. Explain why X may be non-polar overall.  
b. Explain why Y may be polar overall.  
c. State why bond polarity alone is insufficient to determine molecular polarity.

*Suggested marking guide (5):*
- a. Equal, opposite bond dipoles cancel (2).
- b. Bent geometry means the bond dipoles do not cancel completely (2).
- c. Molecular geometry and vector addition/cancellation of bond dipoles must be considered (1).

## F. Suggested formative assessment

- **Entry check:** 5 questions on atomic number, shells, valence electrons and ions.
- **During lessons:** one explanation prompt after each trend; one electron-configuration or Lewis-structure check; one misconception poll.
- **Exit ticket:** learners explain one trend using at least two of nuclear charge, shielding and distance.
- **Retrieval practice:** revisit trend comparisons and bonding distinctions after 1 day, 1 week and 3 weeks.
- **Remediation:** assign a short targeted lesson based on the error (definition, trend explanation, electron arrangement, shape or polarity), not simply another full chapter test.

## G. Practical, model and digital activity ideas

1. **Periodic-trend card sort:** learners arrange element cards and justify trends from supplied atomic numbers and configurations.
2. **Isoelectronic radius comparison:** use a teacher-provided data table; learners explain the effect of proton number for species with equal electron counts.
3. **Lewis-structure workshop:** learners draw simple structures on paper or in a teacher-approved digital whiteboard and peer-check valence-electron counts.
4. **Molecular-shape models:** use clean, reusable classroom models or a vetted simulation. Clearly label models as representations, not exact scale drawings.
5. **Conductivity data interpretation:** use a pre-prepared teacher dataset or a teacher-supervised, institution-approved demonstration. Do not ask learners to handle mains electricity or prepare chemical solutions without the approved laboratory SOP.

No chemical mixing, heating, gas generation or hazardous wet-lab procedure is specified in this pack. Any real laboratory activity must be selected and supervised by a qualified teacher under current institutional risk assessment, PPE, emergency and waste-disposal procedures.
