# HSC 2027 Chemistry — Chapter 1 Teacher Pack
## 1st Paper, Chapter 1: Safe Use of Laboratory

**Status:** Original draft for teacher review. Not an official NCTB chapter outline. Verify every term and required practical against the current prescribed textbook and the academy's laboratory rules before use.

## A. Chapter delivery specification

**Suggested duration:** 2–3 teaching weeks, adjustable to the school's timetable.  
**Prerequisites:** Basic science classroom rules, common measurement units, and the ability to read simple labels.  
**Core outcome:** Learners identify common laboratory hazards, choose appropriate protective measures, use basic apparatus responsibly, record observations accurately, and explain why unsafe actions must be stopped and reported.

### Measurable learning outcomes
By the end of the chapter, a learner can:
1. Distinguish a hazard from a risk and describe a simple risk-control measure.
2. Recognise common hazard pictograms when they appear on a chemical label.
3. Select suitable personal protective equipment for a teacher-approved classroom task.
4. Identify common glassware and measuring apparatus by purpose.
5. Explain why apparatus must be checked before use and why damaged items must be reported.
6. Record a measurement with its unit and distinguish an observation from an inference.
7. Explain the local procedure for reporting spills, injuries, breakages and other incidents.
8. Describe why chemical waste must follow the teacher's disposal instructions.

## B. Lesson sequence

| Lesson | Focus | Teaching and learning activity | Evidence of learning |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Laboratory conduct and responsibility | Establish class rules; learners classify safe/unsafe classroom scenarios | Scenario response |
| 2 | Hazard labels and risk | Interpret teacher-provided label examples; distinguish hazard from risk | Label-matching quiz |
| 3 | PPE and safe behaviour | Match goggles, coat, gloves and footwear to approved tasks; discuss limitations | PPE justification |
| 4 | Apparatus recognition | Identify beakers, measuring cylinders, pipettes, burettes, test tubes and balances from teacher-approved images | Apparatus-purpose table |
| 5 | Measurement and records | Read example scales; record values with units; separate observation from inference | Notebook exercise |
| 6 | Emergency awareness and waste | Learn the institution's reporting and evacuation procedures; identify why unknown waste must not be poured away | Short-answer check |
| 7 | Consolidation | Review misconceptions and complete a mixed formative assessment | Chapter quiz |

**Teaching boundary:** Do not ask learners to mix unknown chemicals, smell chemicals directly, taste substances, heat sealed containers, handle concentrated/corrosive/toxic reagents, or perform spill clean-up. Actual experiments and emergency responses must follow the institution's approved SOPs under qualified supervision. This pack is not an emergency-response manual.

## C. Key concepts and teacher notes

- **Hazard:** an intrinsic property or condition that can cause harm.
- **Risk:** the likelihood and potential severity of harm in a particular situation.
- **Control measure:** an action that removes or reduces exposure to a hazard.
- **Observation:** what is directly seen, measured or otherwise recorded.
- **Inference:** an interpretation drawn from observations; it should not be recorded as though it were directly observed.
- **PPE:** protective equipment selected for the task and used as instructed. PPE does not make an otherwise prohibited task safe.
- **Chemical label:** use the exact label and safety information supplied by the institution; never guess a chemical's identity from appearance.

### Common misconceptions to address
1. “A small amount is always safe.” Amount alone does not determine risk.
2. “Gloves make every chemical safe to handle.” Glove material and task suitability matter; follow the teacher's instructions.
3. “A clear liquid is water.” Appearance does not establish identity.
4. “Smelling a substance is a safe identification test.” Learners must never directly smell unknown chemicals.
5. “An inference is an observation.” Keep measured/seen facts separate from explanations.
6. “All waste can go down the sink.” Waste disposal must follow the laboratory's approved procedure.

## D. Original formative question bank

### Multiple-choice questions

**1. Which statement best describes a hazard?**  
A. The probability that an incident will occur  
B. A property or condition that can cause harm  
C. The cost of laboratory equipment  
D. The result of a completed experiment  
**Answer: B.** A hazard is the source or potential cause of harm; risk considers likelihood and severity in context.

**2. A learner notices a crack in a glass measuring cylinder. What is the best action?**  
A. Use it carefully  
B. Cover the crack with paper  
C. Stop using it and inform the teacher  
D. Transfer the liquid quickly  
**Answer: C.** Damaged glassware must be removed from use and reported under the laboratory procedure.

**3. Which is an observation rather than an inference?**  
A. “A gas was produced because the reaction was fast.”  
B. “The sample is definitely an acid.”  
C. “The liquid changed from colourless to blue.”  
D. “The substance contains copper.”  
**Answer: C.** It describes a directly observed colour change without assigning a cause.

**4. Why should chemical containers remain properly labelled?**  
A. To make shelves look organised  
B. To identify contents and communicate hazards  
C. To make chemicals react faster  
D. To avoid recording measurements  
**Answer: B.** Labels support identification, safe handling and hazard communication.

**5. What should a learner do if an unknown liquid spills?**  
A. Wipe it with bare hands  
B. Smell it to identify it  
C. Follow the local incident procedure and alert the teacher immediately  
D. Pour water on it without asking  
**Answer: C.** Learners should not attempt unapproved clean-up or identification.

**6. Which record is most complete for a measured volume?**  
A. 25  
B. 25 ml approximately  
C. 25 mL  
D. volume is twenty-five  
**Answer: C.** A measurement should include a numerical value and an appropriate unit; precision should match the instrument.

**7. Which statement about PPE is correct?**  
A. PPE permits any experiment  
B. PPE replaces supervision  
C. PPE is selected for the task but does not replace safe procedures  
D. PPE is needed only after an accident  
**Answer: C.** PPE is one part of risk control, not permission to perform unsafe work.

**8. What is the safest way to identify an unknown chemical in a teaching laboratory?**  
A. Taste a tiny amount  
B. Smell it directly  
C. Guess from its colour  
D. Do not use it; alert the teacher and follow the approved identification procedure  
**Answer: D.** Unknown chemicals must not be tasted, directly smelled or handled without authorisation.

### Short-answer questions

**9. Distinguish hazard and risk.**  
Expected points: hazard is a potential source of harm; risk is the likelihood and severity of harm under specific exposure conditions. Give one simple classroom example.

**10. State four items of information that a learner should record in a practical notebook.**  
Expected points: date/title or aim, apparatus/materials as instructed, method as authorised, direct observations, measurements with units, and a clear result/conclusion. Accept any four appropriate items.

**11. Explain why an observation should be separated from an inference.**  
Expected points: observations are recorded evidence; inferences are interpretations; separating them makes results traceable and prevents assumptions from being mistaken for facts.

**12. Give three actions a learner should take before beginning an authorised practical activity.**  
Expected points: read/listen to the procedure, identify hazards and required controls, wear instructed PPE, check apparatus condition, understand the teacher's stop/report instructions. Accept any three.

## E. Application / creative-question practice

**13. Scenario:** During a teacher-supervised demonstration, a learner notices that a reagent bottle has no readable label. Another learner suggests identifying it by smelling it.

**Tasks:**  
a) Identify two hazards in the situation.  
b) State the immediate safe action.  
c) Explain why smell and appearance cannot reliably identify an unknown reagent.  
d) Describe how the incident should be documented according to the institution's procedure.

**Marking guide (10 marks, suggested only):**  
- a) 2 marks: unknown chemical identity and possible exposure/incorrect use  
- b) 2 marks: do not open/use/smell; alert teacher; isolate/leave handling to authorised staff  
- c) 3 marks: sensory properties are unreliable; direct inhalation may expose the learner; identity requires approved documentation/procedure  
- d) 3 marks: record factual details such as time, location, label condition and actions taken; follow local reporting process without guessing identity

**14. Scenario:** A learner records “the solution became hot, therefore a dangerous reaction occurred.” The only measured fact is that the temperature rose from 24 °C to 31 °C.

**Tasks:**  
a) Rewrite the observation accurately.  
b) Identify the inference in the learner's original statement.  
c) Explain what additional information a teacher would need before judging the result.

**Marking guide (6 marks, suggested only):**  
- a) “The recorded temperature increased from 24 °C to 31 °C.” (2)  
- b) “A dangerous reaction occurred” is an unsupported inference. (2)  
- c) Context, approved procedure, substance identity, instrument reliability and teacher assessment are needed. (2)

## F. Practical and assessment design

Use low-risk, teacher-approved activities such as:
- Apparatus identification using photographs or clean, empty equipment.
- Hazard-label interpretation using printed examples.
- Measurement-reading exercises using diagrams or simulated scales.
- Notebook-writing exercises using supplied sample data.
- Incident-reporting role-play without real chemicals or simulated dangerous acts.

Do not turn this chapter into an unsupervised chemical-handling activity. Any real practical must be separately approved by the laboratory teacher with a documented risk assessment and local SOP.

### Suggested chapter assessment blueprint
- 8 MCQs from the bank above (8 marks)
- 2 short-answer questions selected by the teacher (4 marks)
- 1 scenario question (6 marks)
- 1 notebook/apparatus interpretation task (2 marks)
- Total: 20 marks as an internal formative check only; not an official board pattern.
